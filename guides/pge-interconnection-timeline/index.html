How long does it actually take?

Most East Bay projects run 30 to 45 days from signing to permission to operate. The install itself is usually one day. Everything else is paperwork and waiting.

Last updated: September 16, 2026

The stages, in order

  1. Site assessment and design (a few days). Measurements, shade analysis, a look inside your main panel, and a layout. If your electrical service needs work, this is when it surfaces — not later, if whoever designs it is doing their job.
  2. Permit submission (1–3 weeks, jurisdiction dependent). Plans go to your city's building department for structural and electrical review. This is the stage with the most variance, because it depends entirely on which city you're in.
  3. Installation (usually 1 day, sometimes 2–3). Racking, modules, inverter, conduit, and tie-in. Bigger or multi-plane arrays, a panel upgrade, or a battery push this longer.
  4. Inspection (days to a couple of weeks). A city inspector signs off. A failed inspection over something small can cost a week waiting for the next slot, which is why the details matter.
  5. Utility interconnection and PTO (1–4 weeks). The application goes to PG&E, and you wait for permission to operate. You may not legally switch the system on before this arrives.

The part people find maddening: your panels can be fully installed, inspected, and sitting on your roof doing nothing for weeks while you wait on PTO. That's normal. Turning the system on early risks your interconnection agreement, so don't.

Where projects actually get stuck

  • Panel upgrades. If your main service needs replacing, that's a separate permit and a utility disconnect and reconnect. It's the single most common reason a 35-day project becomes a 70-day one.
  • Incomplete permit submissions. A missing structural calculation or an out-of-date form means a correction cycle, and you go back into the queue rather than to the front of it.
  • Historic or design review. Some jurisdictions add a review step. Piedmont is the one East Bay homeowners ask me about most.
  • HOA approval. California law limits what an HOA can refuse, but they can still consume weeks. Start that conversation early.
  • Roof work discovered late. If the roof needs replacing, everything stops. This is why I look at the roof on the first visit.

It varies by city

Permitting is the least predictable stage, and it's local. California's SB 379 pushed cities toward automated instant permitting for straightforward residential systems, and where it's genuinely running it removes most of the plan-review wait. Adoption across the East Bay is uneven, so what applies to your address depends on your address.

I keep track of which counters are moving and which aren't, and I handle the submissions. You shouldn't have to learn your city's permit portal to put panels on your own house. What I can tell you by city: Oakland, Berkeley, Alameda, San Leandro.

How to make it go faster

  • Get twelve months of PG&E usage data ready before the first visit.
  • Say early if you're planning an EV, heat pump, or addition — it changes the design, and changing it later restarts permitting.
  • Deal with the roof first if it's near end of life.
  • Start any HOA conversation the week you sign, not after.
  • Don't wait on a rebate that may not come; decide on the economics without it.

One timing note worth knowing: California's solar property tax exclusion is scheduled to sunset January 1, 2027, and it applies to systems completed before then. With a 30-to-45-day runway, that deadline is closer than it looks. More on that in the NEM 3 guide.
