What solar actually costs in Oakland.

Nobody wants to book three consultations to find out the price. Here is the range I see on real East Bay projects, and the things that move a quote up or down.

Last updated: September 16, 2026

Read this first. Any price you see online, mine included, is a range and not a quote. Roof pitch, panel location, shade, and the state of your electrical service move the number by thousands. What follows is meant to tell you whether a quote you've been handed is in the realm of reason — not to replace looking at your actual house.

How solar is priced

Almost everyone quotes in dollars per watt before incentives. A system is described by its size in kilowatts — a 6 kW system is 6,000 watts — and the installed price is that size times a per-watt rate that covers panels, inverter, racking, wiring, labor, permits, and the installer's margin.

Per-watt pricing is useful because it lets you compare quotes for different system sizes. It's also where quotes get slippery: a low per-watt number on an oversized system can cost more than a fair rate on a right-sized one. Compare total installed price and the rate, and make sure you're comparing systems designed to do the same job.

What moves the number

Two houses on the same Oakland block can differ by ten thousand dollars. The usual reasons:

  • Roof type and pitch. Composition shingle on a walkable pitch is the cheap case. Spanish tile, slate, or anything steep enough to need fall protection costs more in labor and breakage.
  • How many planes. One big south-facing rectangle is cheap. Six small facets facing five directions — common on Oakland Victorians and Craftsman bungalows — means more racking, more penetrations, more hours.
  • Your main panel. A modern 200-amp panel with room is fine. A 100-amp panel, a subpanel wired oddly, or a Federal Pacific or Zinsco that needs replacing adds real cost before a single module goes up.
  • Conduit run. The distance from array to panel matters, and detached garages are expensive.
  • Shade mitigation. Microinverters or optimizers cost more than a string inverter but recover production on partly shaded roofs. Most East Bay roofs have some shade, so this is usually the right spend.
  • Storage. A battery is typically the largest single line after the array itself.

Reading a quote honestly

Things I'd want to see before signing anything:

  • The total installed price in dollars, not just a monthly payment. A monthly figure hides both the price and the finance charge.
  • Specific equipment — manufacturer and model for panels, inverter, and battery. "Premium tier-1 panels" is not a spec.
  • A production estimate in kilowatt-hours per year, with the shade assumptions behind it.
  • Who actually installs it, who holds the license, and who you call in year six. Plenty of well-known names subcontract the work.
  • Warranty terms separated out: panels, inverter, battery, and workmanship are four different promises of four different lengths from four different parties.
  • The finance charge, if financed. A "$0 down" offer with a large dealer fee baked into the price is a real cost, and it is often thousands.

The 2026 caveat on every savings estimate

If a proposal shows a 30% federal tax credit, it is wrong. That credit ended for homeowner-purchased systems on December 31, 2025. I've seen proposals this year still carrying it, sometimes from sloppiness and sometimes not. Either way, a savings projection built on it overstates your return by a large margin. The detail is in the NEM 3 guide.

The cheapest quote usually isn't

I've spent a lot of time on roofs fixing systems that were the low bid. The pattern is consistent: thin racking, sloppy flashing that leaks in the second winter, undersized conduit, no monitoring, and a company that has since changed names. Repairing that costs more than the original difference.

I'm not arguing for the most expensive quote either. I'm arguing for understanding what the gap between two numbers actually buys. That's most of what I do — and if the fair quote is from someone else, I'll say so.
