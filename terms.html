The consultation

The site visit is free, roughly 45 minutes, and carries no obligation on either side. Requesting one doesn't commit you to buying anything, and it doesn't commit me to taking on your project.

What you get is my honest read on your home, which sometimes means recommending a smaller system than you expected, or recommending against solar altogether. That's the point of the visit. To give you a useful answer, I need accurate information from you about your usage, your roof, and your plans — my analysis is only as good as what I'm working from.

Get in touch by email, phone, or text to arrange a visit. If you need to reschedule, let me know as early as you can.

Estimates, savings projections, and incentives

Any cost or savings figure I give you — verbal, written, or in a proposal — is an estimate built on assumptions. Those assumptions include your historical electricity usage, your current rate plan, expected utility rate increases, system production modeling, and current incentive rules. Every one of them can change, and some certainly will over the life of a system.

A few specifics worth stating plainly:

  • Production estimates are models, not measurements. Actual output varies with weather, shading, soiling, and equipment performance.
  • Utility rates are outside anyone's control. Projections that run twenty or thirty years out depend on PG&E rate assumptions that no one can guarantee.
  • Tax credits depend on your tax situation, not mine. Whether you can use the federal solar tax credit — and how much of it — depends on your tax liability and circumstances. I'm not a tax professional. Talk to yours before counting on a credit.
  • Rebates and programs have deadlines and funding caps and can close without much warning.

Actual pricing and terms come from the signed contract for your project, not from an estimate or a conversation.

Installation work and contractors

Depending on the project, installation, electrical, and roofing work may be performed by me or by independent licensed contractors I coordinate with. The written contract for your project identifies who is performing the work and who is responsible for it, and that document controls.

Where an independent contractor performs the work, that contractor is responsible for their own workmanship, licensing, insurance, permits, and warranty obligations. Referring you to a contractor I trust is not a guarantee of their work, and it doesn't make me a party to your contract with them.

In California, home improvement contracts over $500 must be in writing, and you generally have a three-day right to cancel — three business days, or longer for some homeowners, including those aged 65 and over. Your contract will spell this out. You can verify any contractor's license at the California State License Board, and I'd encourage you to actually do it — for anyone you're considering, including anyone I recommend.

Equipment warranties

Panels, inverters, and batteries carry warranties from their manufacturers, and installation work carries a workmanship warranty from whoever installed it. Those are the warranties that cover your system. I'm not the guarantor of any of them, and a manufacturer going out of business is a real risk in this industry — one worth weighing when you choose equipment. I'll help you understand what's covered and help you pursue a claim, but the obligation sits with the manufacturer or installer.

The quotes on this site are from real customers describing their own experience. They're not a prediction of your results. Someone else's savings depended on their roof, their usage, and the rules in effect when they installed. Yours will be different.
